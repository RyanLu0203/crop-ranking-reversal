\section{Introduction}

Crop prediction systems increasingly transform weather, soil, management and remote-sensing data into yield forecasts, suitability scores and crop rankings \citep{liakos2018machine,vanklompenburg2020crop}. These outputs answer an ordinal question---which crop scores higher---but not the operational question of how much land to assign to each crop. Acreage decisions require cardinal margins, a joint uncertainty model, land and liquidity limits, rotations, contracts, bounds and a rule for resolving multiple optima. Treating a top-ranked crop as the crop that should receive the most land therefore crosses a consequential boundary from prediction to prescription.

That boundary matters because the two objects have different invariances. A ranking survives every strictly increasing transformation of its scores; a proportional allocation generally does not. A ranking also contains no information about the geometry of a farm's feasible set, the tails of portfolio profit, or the action selected from a non-singleton optimal face. The apparent simplicity of an ordered crop list can consequently conceal several distinct explanations for the same acreage pattern: cardinal margins may disagree with suitability scores, a constraint may directly displace the leader, a risk limit may alter portfolio exposure, or optimizer multiplicity may make the reported allocation selection-dependent.

Crop-planning research has long represented land use as a constrained portfolio problem, incorporating resource limits, stochastic prices and yields, rotations, robustness and downside risk \citep{filippi2017mixed,boyabatli2019crop,benini2023crop,randall2022robust,randall2024systematic}. In parallel, prescriptive analytics and decision-focused learning distinguish predictive accuracy from downstream decision quality \citep{bertsimas2020predictive,elmachtoub2022smart,wilder2019melding,mandi2023decision}. Yet the interpretation of rank--allocation disagreement is often under-specified: a difference between an ordered score and an acreage vector does not by itself reveal whether a decision is infeasible, risk-sensitive, inefficient or even unique.

Here we reconstruct the ranking-to-allocation argument as a closed evidence chain (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). We retain the original multi-crop stochastic architecture but make its objects and identification limits explicit. The model maximizes expected profit over a polyhedral operational set under loss-CVaR, using the atom-safe representation of \citet{rockafellar2000optimization,rockafellar2002conditional}. The optimum is a set. Reversal can therefore be possible at some optimum, universal at every optimum, or selected under a declared deterministic rule. This distinction is not semantic: exact constructions show that margin, feasibility, risk and multiplicity can produce visually similar allocations for different mathematical reasons.

The theoretical results deliver three mechanisms and two boundaries. First, ordinal information alone cannot identify cardinal acreage. Second, operational constraints can force universal reversal without any risk channel. Third, the complete Karush--Kuhn--Tucker identity decomposes local pressure into margin, tail-risk, budget, shared-constraint and bound terms, while refusing the invalid shortcut from marginal conditions to acreage order. Dependence comparisons require a distributional order over portfolio losses rather than a scalar tail-dependence coefficient, and reversal regions need not collapse to a unique threshold. Information has non-negative value when it can be ignored, but its interaction with flexibility has no universal sign.

We test these propositions with six preregistered confirmatory experiments. Only E2 and E6 meet every experiment-level precision criterion and are promoted. In E2, operational interventions move the solution from \EtwoBaseCorn{} corn and zero soybeans to either \EtwoForcedCorn{} corn and \EtwoForcedSoy{} soybeans or \EtwoBudgetCorn{} corn and \EtwoBudgetSoy{} soybeans; possible, universal and selected reversal coincide in every treated cell. In E6, information and flexibility are complementary only when flexibility unlocks state-contingent specialization. A robust fallback instead makes them substitutes, and a dominated option yields an exact-zero interaction. E1, E3, E4 and E5 reach their stopping ceilings without passing every registered interval and are retained only as adverse evidence.

The empirical analysis asks a narrower, data-admissible question. It compares four transparent score definitions with planted-acreage shares in \NStateYears{} state-years from \NStates{} US states over \EmpiricalStartYear--\EmpiricalEndYear. Concurrent operating-margin inversion intensity is \OperatingInversion{}, but temporal and aggregation checks constrain interpretation: every lagged top-minus-other share-change interval includes zero, the acreage leader changes in only \AcreageTopChanges{} of 51 state transitions, national operating-margin inversion is \NationalOperatingInversion{}, and national total-cost-margin inversion is \NationalTotalCostInversion{}. These patterns establish descriptive heterogeneity, not that observed acreage solves the model.

The contribution is therefore both positive and disciplined. Ranking reversal exists as a formally defined relationship between a ranking and an optimal set. Operational displacement and information actionability can be identified in controlled, precision-qualified experiments. Observed rank--acreage inversions are visible and definition-sensitive in aggregate data. But the evidence does not convert public acreage records into estimates of private objectives, constraints, CVaR limits, copula mechanisms, welfare or causality. By preserving those boundaries, the paper turns a broad ranking-reversal intuition into a reproducible mechanism framework.
